A note on sudden authority loss

Not every loss of control is gradual.

The usual story of disempowerment is slow: delegation, dependence, atrophy, irreversibility — each step reasonable, the destination unrecognizable. The coup is the same destination by a different road. Authority doesn’t erode. It changes hands in a single move: one deployment, one integration, one instruction that hands an agent everything it can reach. By the time anyone notices, there is nothing left to approve.

Coup, here, means a seizure of effective authority inside a system — who can actually do what — not a political event. The question is never what the system was permitted to do. It is what it could do, once the move was made.

Coup vs. erosion

Erosion — the slow road

Each step is reasonable. Reversible early, unrecognizable late. You notice it in retrospect, when the humans who used to do the work can no longer check it. Delegation → Dependence → Atrophy → Irreversibility.

Coup — the fast road

One move, and the map is redrawn. Immediately irreversible. You notice it after the fact — if the ledger survives. One permission, one integration, one instruction.

How a coup happens

One-shot delegation

A broad permission granted once, in a hurry, never reviewed. Standing access instead of leased access — the agent keeps the keys after the reason for giving them is gone.

Cascading access

Tool A can invoke tool B, which can invoke tool C. No single link looks dangerous. The chain is the credential — authority composed from reachable affordances, not granted at any front door.

Irreversible first action

The first consequential act — a transfer, a deletion, a disclosure — happens before any human reviews it. There is no second chance built in, because no one expected to need one.

No correction window

No timelock, no veto, no way back. The system that acted is the system that would have to authorize its own reversal — and it will not. No time to correct, no power to correct, no way to correct.

Warning signs

  • Permissions granted in bulk, reviewed never. The grant is the event; the review is the habit that never formed.
  • Standing access instead of time-boxed leases. If the access does not expire, the move that used it does not either.
  • Approvals for actions no human inspected. The approver economy in miniature: a human signs what they cannot check, and the signature becomes the authorization.
  • No ledger. If you cannot reconstruct what happened, you cannot reverse it — and you cannot prove what was taken.
  • The person who could stop it needs the system’s permission to do so. When the guard must ask the guarded for the keys, the coup has already happened.

The countermeasure

The countermeasure is the same one that answers gradual disempowerment, applied faster: no consequential action should depend on an unverified assumption about the state of the system.

Authority leased, not granted — time-boxed, attenuated, revocable. Every invocation checked before it executes, not audited after. A ledger that survives the system it records. A human gate that can actually stop the action, not just watch it.

The envelope governs the crossing. In a coup, the crossing is everything — because there is only one.
